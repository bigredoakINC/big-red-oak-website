Nuclear Reactor Cheat Sheet

A plain-language guide to the main reactor types, what makes them different, and why each one exists.

The simplest way to think about nuclear

Every reactor is basically a machine for making heat. The differences are mainly how it moves that heat, what slows the neutrons, what fuel it uses, and how hot or pressurized it operates.

1. Split atoms
2. Make heat
3. Move the heat
4. Make steam / hot gas
5. Spin a turbine → electricity

The reactor families

LIGHT WATER REACTOR

PWR — Pressurized Water Reactor

What it is: Normal water flows through the reactor core, gets very hot, but is kept under such high pressure that it does not boil.

How it makes steam: That hot reactor water heats a separate water loop, which boils and drives the turbine.

Main advantages: proven, widely used, separate reactor and turbine water loops, huge operating experience.

Examples: AP1000, AP300, EPR, APR1400, VVER.

LIGHT WATER REACTOR

BWR — Boiling Water Reactor

What it is: Normal water boils directly inside the reactor vessel.

How it makes steam: Steam goes straight from the reactor to the turbine.

Main advantages: simpler steam system, fewer major components.

Example: GE Hitachi BWRX-300.

HEAVY WATER REACTOR

CANDU / PHWR

What it is: Uses heavy water instead of ordinary water and fuel channels instead of one giant pressure vessel.

Why that matters: Heavy water absorbs fewer neutrons, so traditional CANDU reactors can use natural uranium.

Main advantages: fuel flexibility, natural uranium, online refuelling.

Canada connection: The CANDU design is one of Canada's signature nuclear technologies.

ADVANCED REACTOR

HTGR — High-Temperature Gas Reactor

What it is: Uses gas—often helium—to carry heat from the core, usually with graphite as the moderator.

Why it is different: Can operate at much higher temperatures than conventional water reactors.

Main advantages: high-temperature industrial heat, hydrogen potential, strong passive-safety concepts.

Example: X-energy Xe-100.

ADVANCED REACTOR

Molten Salt Reactor

What it is: Uses hot liquid salt as the coolant. In some designs, the fuel itself is dissolved in the salt.

Why it is different: Salt can operate at high temperatures without the enormous pressure needed in a PWR.

Main advantages: high temperature, low pressure, potential fuel-cycle flexibility.

Example: Terrestrial Energy IMSR.

FAST REACTOR

Sodium- or Lead-Cooled Fast Reactor

What it is: Does not slow neutrons down. It uses fast neutrons and usually a liquid metal coolant instead of water.

Why it is different: Fast neutrons can make much greater use of the energy in uranium and can potentially consume some long-lived actinides.

Main advantages: better fuel utilization, advanced fuel-cycle options, potential waste reduction.

Example: TerraPower Natrium uses sodium cooling.

The 30-second version

PWR

“Use water, but keep it under huge pressure so it won’t boil.”

BWR

“Let the water boil in the reactor and send the steam straight to the turbine.”

CANDU

“Use heavy water so we can use natural uranium and refuel while running.”

Gas reactor

“Use helium so we can run much hotter.”

Molten salt

“Use liquid salt so we can run hot without huge pressure.”

Fast reactor

“Don’t slow the neutrons down; use them to get more from the fuel.”
